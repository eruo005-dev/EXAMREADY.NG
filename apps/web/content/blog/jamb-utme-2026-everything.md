JAMB UTME 2026 is the gateway to a Nigerian university for almost everyone reading this. Get the basics right and you remove most of the avoidable stress between now and exam day.

This guide covers what JAMB actually tests, the registration window, the timetable rhythm, scoring, and a realistic study plan calibrated to where you are right now.

> **Verify the exact dates on the JAMB official portal before relying on them for planning.** JAMB historically publishes the registration window in late January / early February for the April-May exam window, but the dates do shift. The first authoritative source is always [jamb.gov.ng](https://www.jamb.gov.ng/).

## What JAMB UTME tests

JAMB is computer-based (CBT). You take **four subjects** in a single 2-hour session:

1. **English Language** — compulsory for everyone
2. **Three subjects relevant to your intended course of study** — picked from a list

Each subject delivers 40 multiple-choice questions, totalling 160 questions in 120 minutes. That's an average of 45 seconds per question, including time to read. **Speed is half the exam.**

The combination matters. If you want to study Medicine you need Biology, Chemistry, Physics + English. If you want to study Law you need Literature, Government, History (or another arts/social-science combo) + English. The full subject-combination map is on [our subject combinations tool](/tools/subject-combinations).

### What changed for 2026

A few details to verify on the JAMB portal:

- The use of CBT centres approved by JAMB (not your school)
- The biometric verification on exam day — make sure your fingerprints register cleanly during registration; failure to verify on exam day is a disqualification risk
- Mock exams (the "JAMB Mock") run a few weeks before the main exam; they're optional but useful

## The registration timeline

Realistic timeline based on prior years (verify exact dates):

| Phase         | When (typical) | What happens                                                                            |
| ------------- | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ePIN purchase | February       | Go to a CBT-approved centre, pay registration fee, get your ePIN                        |
| Registration  | February-March | Use the ePIN on the JAMB portal; choose subject combo, university preferences (up to 4) |
| Mock exam     | March-April    | Optional but useful — same UI as the real exam                                          |
| Main exam     | April-May      | Multiple sessions over ~3 weeks; you don't pick your slot                               |
| Results       | May-June       | Released in batches, accessible via SMS or portal                                       |

**The most expensive mistake students make:** missing the registration window because they thought there was time. JAMB does not extend deadlines for personal reasons. As soon as registration opens, register that week.

## Scoring

JAMB UTME scores from 0-400. The breakdown:

- 4 subjects × 40 questions = 160 questions
- Each question is worth 2.5 marks
- 160 × 2.5 = 400 max

What you actually need depends on the university and course:

- **Medicine, Pharmacy, Law at top federal universities (UI, UNILAG, OAU, ABU):** 280+ realistically. Top departments cut at 290-310 in some years.
- **Engineering, Sciences at federal universities:** 240-280 range
- **Arts, social sciences at federal:** 200-240
- **State universities:** generally 180-220 cut-offs
- **Private universities:** 180+ accepted at most, lower at some

JAMB sets a national cut-off (usually 140-160), but **universities can and do set their own higher cut-offs**. The national score is a floor, not a target. See [our cut-off marks tool](/tools/cutoff-marks) for the most recent published cut-offs by university and course.

## A realistic preparation timeline

The honest answer to "how should I study" depends on how much time you have. Here are three realistic plans:

### 6+ months until your exam (start now if you're reading this in 2025)

This is the comfortable timeline. Study 8-10 hours per week split across:

- **3 hours daily practice** (40 minutes per subject × 4) — focus on weak topics
- **2 hours weekly mock CBT** — full timed paper, simulate the real thing
- **2 hours weekly review** — go over wrong answers, understand why

Strong students cover the full syllabus by month 3 and spend months 4-6 on past papers. Weaker students spend months 1-3 on fundamentals before any past papers.

### 3-4 months until exam

Tighter. 12-15 hours per week:

- Skip topics that historically appear less than 5% of the time (you'll see 1-2 such questions max — not worth the time)
- 4 mock exams in the final 2 months — simulate the pressure
- Past papers from 2019-2024 in topic order, then in random order in the final month

[Our practice mode](/practice) tags every question by topic + frequency score so you can prioritise.

### < 6 weeks until exam

This is salvage mode. 20+ hours per week, primarily:

- Past papers, past papers, past papers — 5+ full mock exams
- Timing drills — the slowest 25% of students fail because they didn't finish, not because they didn't know
- Don't try to learn new fundamentals; reinforce what you already half-know

## Practical tips that move scores

These are the things students most often skip:

1. **Practice on a laptop or desktop, not a phone, at least once a week.** The exam is on a computer; if you've only ever practised on a phone, the keyboard layout and screen size will throw off your timing.
2. **Time yourself. Always.** Don't practise without a timer. The single most valuable habit is "set 30 minutes, do 20 questions, score honestly."
3. **Read the entire question.** JAMB writes traps: "all of the following EXCEPT" or "find the WRONG statement." Skimming wastes points students could have collected.
4. **For comprehension passages, read the questions FIRST.** Then read the passage looking for those answers. It's roughly 2x faster than reading the whole passage twice.
5. **For maths and physics, write your working on the scratch paper provided.** Don't try to do everything mentally — that's where calculation errors come from.
